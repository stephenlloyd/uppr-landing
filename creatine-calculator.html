What creatine is, in one paragraph

Your muscles run short, hard efforts on a fast-recharging fuel called phosphocreatine — it slaps a phosphate back onto spent ATP so you can keep firing. You make some yourself and get some from meat and fish, but your muscle stores aren't full by default. Supplementing creatine monohydrate tops them up, which means a little more in the tank for those last reps — and across weeks, that extra work adds up to measurably more strength and lean mass. That's it. No stimulant, no hormone, no trick.

What it actually does (and what it doesn't)

  • Strength and power: consistently the largest, most replicated effect — meta-analyses put resistance-training strength gains roughly 8–14% above training alone.
  • Lean mass: a real, modest bump over time — partly more training volume tolerated, partly intracellular water (which is the point: hydrated muscle cells are an anabolic signal, not "bloat").
  • Recovery & high-rep work: better performance on repeated bouts; helps you do more total work per session.
  • Brain: a smaller but real literature on cognitive benefits, most noticeable when you're sleep-deprived or under stress.
  • It won't make you fat, dehydrate you, cramp you up, or take your hair — see below.

How much, and whether to load

The ISSN's position stand is unambiguous: 3–5 g of creatine monohydrate per day, every day, is all you need. We use 5 g as the headline because it's cheap, removes the guesswork, and covers larger lifters comfortably.

Loading is optional. You can do ~0.3 g per kg of bodyweight per day, split into four doses, for 5–7 days, then drop to the daily dose — your stores fill in about a week. Skip it and just take 5 g/day from the start, and you reach the exact same muscle saturation in roughly three to four weeks (Hultman's 1996 study showed 3 g/day for 28 days matched 20 g/day for 5 days). Same destination; loading just gets there faster. The calculator shows you both.

Timing doesn't matter — consistency does. Morning, with food, post-workout, doesn't matter; missing it for a week does (you'll slowly drift back down). Make it a daily habit and you can stop thinking about it.

Use plain creatine monohydrate. The "advanced" forms (HCl, ethyl ester, buffered, "nitrate") are more expensive and have no evidence of being better — monohydrate is the form essentially every study used. Look for Creapure® if you want a quality marker; it's optional.

Things you've heard that aren't true

"Creatine makes you bloated / hold water."

There's a small jump in intracellular water — inside the muscle cell — in the first week or two. That's the mechanism doing its job, not subcutaneous bloat. Long-term studies don't show a body-water problem.

"It damages your kidneys or liver."

In healthy people, no — this has been studied for decades, including long-term use, with no harm to kidney or liver markers. (Existing kidney disease is a real exception — see the note at the bottom.)

"Creatine causes hair loss."

This comes from one small 2009 study that found a rise in a hormone (DHT) — not in hair loss itself — and it has never been replicated. There's no good evidence creatine causes baldness.

"You need to cycle off it."

No. Your body doesn't down-regulate its own production in any way that matters, and stopping just lets your stores fade back to baseline over a few weeks. Continuous use is fine and is what the research used.
